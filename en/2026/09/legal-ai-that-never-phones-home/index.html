The model can’t leave the laptop: building a legal AI that never phones home

30 September 2026 · writing · history

A Turkish criminal defense file is about as sensitive as a document set gets. Indictment, witness statements, forensic and medical reports, phone extractions, a criminal record: all in one folder, pulled from UYAP (the national judiciary system) as UDF files, PDFs and scanned TIFs. Hundreds of pages.

For most of this year I have been building Tiro with a small team at Lycian. It’s a desktop assistant for criminal defense lawyers. You drop a case file in and ask questions. It builds a timeline and a map of the people and evidence, and it answers with the document and line numbers it relied on. The product line is “Söz aramızda kalır”, roughly “it stays between us”. Engineering-wise, that line is the whole spec: the case file never leaves the lawyer’s computer.

This post is about what that one constraint forced us to design differently.

Why the cloud is off the table

This is not a style preference. Article 36 of the Turkish Attorneys’ Act forbids a lawyer from disclosing anything learned through their work, and it doesn’t carve out exceptions for software. Under KVKK, Turkey’s data protection law, data about criminal convictions and security measures, health and biometrics is “special category” personal data, and a criminal file is full of it. Sending personal data abroad goes through KVKK Article 9, rewritten by Law No. 7499 and in force since 1 June 2024: an adequacy decision, appropriate safeguards such as a notified standard contract, or narrow incidental exceptions. Clicking through a chatbot’s terms of service fits none of them cleanly.

In August 2026 the Union of Turkish Bar Associations (TBB) published AI guidance that puts criminal files in the high-sensitivity group and recommends closed or on-premise systems for them.

Once the model runs on the lawyer’s own machine, the data-transfer question never comes up. What’s left is the security of that machine and the quality of a model small enough to run on it. Everything below comes from that tradeoff.

Decision 1: Privacy by construction, not by configuration

At one point our backend had a clean provider abstraction with local, Anthropic, Gemini and OpenAI adapters. A prod_mode flag locked packaged builds to the local model.

We deleted the remote adapters. The factory now returns the local llama.cpp model unconditionally, in dev as well. A flag can be defeated by one wrong environment variable. A code path that doesn’t exist can’t be selected.

Docs are part of the privacy surface too. Months after the code was gone, our README and .env.example still described an LLM_PROVIDER knob with four API key slots. Read by an auditor, that says case files can go to a remote API. I treated it as a bug.

“Local” also turned out to need more than binding to 127.0.0.1. Our FastAPI backend had allow_origins=["*"] with allow_credentials=True. Starlette doesn’t send a wildcard in that case. It echoes back the requesting origin. So any web page open in the lawyer’s browser could call the local backend and read the reply, because a browser can reach loopback. “Runs on localhost” is not the same as “only the app can talk to it”. The fix was an explicit origin allowlist with credentials off, and the longer-term plan is to serve the Electron renderer from its own dedicated origin so the allowlist can shrink to one entry.

Decision 2: Memory is the architecture

On a 16 GB laptop, the RAM budget decides what the product can do. The default chat model is Gemma 4 E4B at Q4_K_M. Next to it run bge-m3 for embeddings and bge-reranker-v2-m3 as a cross-encoder, all as GGUF through llama-cpp-python, on Metal on macOS and Vulkan on Windows.

The first version of the hardware-aware tier picker only counted the LLM, so it offered 16 GB Macs the Gemma 12B tier. With the embedder, the reranker and the lawyer’s usual apps open, the first chat turn ran out of GPU memory, even at an 8K context. On my own 16 GB machine the 12B eval run failed nearly every question in under a second. Now the recommender counts about 1.3 GB of companion models, and 12B starts at 18 GB.

The failure mode mattered more than the failure itself. A failed Metal command buffer leaves llama.cpp’s backend broken until you recreate it. We cached the model, so every later turn failed right away. Now a decode failure marks the model as poisoned and the next call loads a fresh one.

Concurrency was the other trap: llama.cpp contexts are not thread-safe, and an asyncio.gather over two embeddings caused a silent segfault. The LLM and reranker now share one inference thread and embedding is serialized. Throughput suffers; a crash mid-analysis costs more.

Decision 3: Don’t let a 4B model write its own citations

For a lawyer, the value of an answer is “Document 4, lines 112 to 118”: click it, see the lines highlighted.

Small models are bad at copying line numbers, even with the numbers right there in the context. So the model doesn’t get the final say. After generation, every (Belge N, satır X-Y) (“Document N, lines X-Y”) citation is re-anchored by token overlap to the lines of document N that actually carry the cited sentence. If no lines match, the citation drops to a bare “(Belge N)” rather than showing an invented range. On our demo eval, citations that land on supporting lines went from 4 of 52 to 40 of 54.

The idea we rejected: when a citation didn’t match, move it to the document with the most shared words. In a criminal file, words like “sanık” (defendant), “olay” (incident) and “söyledi” (said) appear in every document. So the best word match elsewhere can be a statement that says the opposite. A citation to the right document without line numbers is safer than a precise citation to the wrong one.

Offline also changes how strict you have to be. A desktop install has no central log and no kill switch. If a bad citation shows up, it goes straight to the lawyer’s screen and I never hear about it. So I raised the fuzzy-match floor from 0.4 to 0.7 token overlap and pulled the score bands apart, so that “exact”, “whitespace-normalized” and “fuzzy” can never tie. For the same reason, swallowed exceptions get logged and CI runs the full suite on every PR: the only rollback for a bad release is a new installer.

Decision 4: Retrieval has to be measured, not assumed

With a small model, retrieval quality sets a ceiling on answer quality. Every change I kept, I measured on two sets: a fictional demo case (78 questions, 64 of them answerable from the file), and a 31-question set built from a real UYAP file. The real set never enters the repo. The eval script takes it by path.

Things the numbers taught me:

  • The embedding model was the bottleneck. After fixing chunking (documents had been truncated at a 512-token window), on a Turkish paraphrase set nomic-embed v1.5 put the right chunk in the top 5 for 25% of questions, barely above random at about 19%. bge-m3 reached 91%.
  • My hybrid weights were hurting. Vector 45, BM25 35, doc-type prior 20 scored below plain vector search (R@1 0.34 vs 0.53). At 0.9 vector and 0.1 BM25 it matched plain vector search (R@1 0.53, R@5 0.88) while keeping literal queries like “TCK 29” still in the top 3.
  • HyDE made things worse. Embedding a hypothetical answer, a well-known retrieval trick, pulled in documents that read like the answer instead of the one that held it. Removing it took the real file from 19 to 25 of 31 correct and saved one LLM call per turn.
  • Lexical search needs its own pool. BM25 only re-ranked what vector search had already returned, so a passage matching the exact words but ranked 22nd by vector never reached the reranker. A case-wide BM25 index that adds its own top 8, including file names (UYAP names files by document type, and lawyers ask by that type), took the real file from 25 to 28 of 31. Median answer time went from 12.4 to 10.0 seconds.
  • Law text needs context to be findable. TCK Article 29’s text never says “haksız tahrik” (unjust provocation), even though that’s the concept it defines. Prefixing each chunk with its article number and title took recall@1 on literal law questions from 0.40 to 1.00.

Decision 5: The fine-tune we stopped recommending

We fine-tuned Qwen3.5 4B with LoRA for structured extraction, trained on anonymized and synthetic documents, and shipped it as the “Tiro Tuned” tier. It sounded like a moat.

Then I measured it on the 9-document demo case. The fine-tune assigned 20 of roughly 40 person roles wrongly, with full confidence, and invented 7 charges. Off-the-shelf Gemma 4 E4B got 2 roles wrong, invented none, and ran at the same speed. Part of that was our JSON grammar forcing a key order it was never trained on; fixing it didn’t close the gap.

It stays selectable by hand, but the recommender skips it. I also corrected our website, which said the app runs a model we trained. It doesn’t: the model is off the shelf. The search, grounding and citation layer is ours. Owning that sentence publicly mattered more to me than the marketing line.

What’s still hard

  • Speed. A median of about 10 seconds per answer on a laptop is fine for research and slow for conversation. OCR of an 88-page scanned TIF took about 15 minutes, so scans now go to the back of the parse queue.
  • Small evals. 64 and 31 questions, with run-to-run noise of about ±2. I compare whole sets, never single questions, and some of our “tried and dropped” ideas simply never cleared the noise.
  • “Offline” has edges. The app needs the internet to download models at setup. Case files never need it. And once the data stays on the laptop, the laptop’s security is the lawyer’s job, so we tell users to turn on FileVault or BitLocker.

Closing

Having no cloud fallback forces more discipline than I expected. You can’t throw a bigger model at a bad retrieval pipeline, so you fix the pipeline. You can’t patch production quietly, so every release is treated as permanent. And the model can’t be trusted with the details that matter, so you verify them after it speaks.

That’s the part of local AI I find most interesting. Running a model on a laptop is the easy part now. What matters is everything you build around a model that is small, runs only on the lawyer’s computer, and is sometimes wrong.
