Making an AI agent do the literature review

18 September 2026 · writing · history

My team and I put together a work plan for my final year project. The first step is that everyone does a thorough literature review on their own topic. I am still new to the academic world, so forgive me in advance if I use a term wrongly.

As far as I understand it so far, the literature review is the part of research that takes the most time and shows the least. You download dozens of papers — sometimes even ones with no direct bearing on the topic — open them one by one, try to read them, and take notes on what matters. All of that has to be kept orderly and systematic, of course. There are plenty of reference managers, note-taking apps and summarising AI tools for the job.

In this post I want to share the agent workflow that, as someone just stepping into academia, made my literature review considerably easier.

What MCP is, and why it matters

MCP (Model Context Protocol) is a protocol that gives an AI agent the ability to use external tools. An agent like Claude Code no longer only produces text: it can open and search a NotebookLM notebook, add a paper to your Zotero library, write your notes. The agent calls the functions behind the interfaces you would otherwise be clicking.

The critical part for me was this: once you connect both tools inside the same session, the “paper → summary → library” chain stops being copy-paste and becomes a single flow.

Step 1 — A separate brain per paper

I set up the NotebookLM MCP first. NotebookLM’s strength is that it grounds its answers only in the sources you upload, and ties every claim back to its place in the text with a clickable citation.

What I did was this: I uploaded each paper I was reviewing into its own notebook. Why separate ones? Because I wanted every paper to be questioned within its own context, without bleeding into the others. When I ask a paper “what is the methodological weakness of this study?”, I do not want the answer leaking in from a different paper.

Step 2 — Consistent summaries: one prompt, every paper

This was the crux of it for me. Instead of saying “summarise this” at random, I used the standard literature-review prompt our supervisor gave us in a team meeting. It produces output with the same shape for every paper:

  • Aim and contribution of the study
  • Methodology and dataset
  • Main findings
  • Limitations and criticisms
  • Relation to our own research

Then I had the agent run that same prompt for every notebook and save the output as an “Akademik Review” note. The result: dozens of papers, comparable with one another, summarised on the same skeleton.

The subject of the review taught me something too: the literature on evaluating LLMs and RAG — ARES, BARTScore, BERTScore, BLEU, BLEURT, and systematic surveys on evaluating LLMs. So, ironically, I reviewed the papers that evaluate AI using AI.

Step 3 — Collecting it all in one library

Once the notes existed, I connected the second piece: the Zotero MCP. Zotero looks to be the standard in academia. Connected over MCP, the agent can add papers to your library by DOI or URL, pull metadata, and manage collections.

The move that paid off most for me: inside the same session, I added all the papers to Zotero and attached the “Akademik Review” notes NotebookLM had produced to each paper. So in my Zotero library every source now carries its PDF, its metadata and an AI summary in a standard format, side by side. Reference management, summary and full text — all three in one place.

What it comes to

What this workflow actually taught me is not “let the AI read the papers for me”. Reading and judging a paper is still mine; what I handed over is the repetitive, error-prone part — collecting, summarising consistently, filing. I automated the drudgery around reading, not the reading.

The mechanical weight of a review that would take a long time by hand drops sharply, and the time left over goes to what actually matters: drawing the connections between the papers.

As I said, I am new to this, so read it as “it worked for me” rather than as settled advice. If you have built a better flow or tried different tools, I would very much like to hear it.
