Three sites in five years

24 July 2026 · writing · history

I built three personal sites in five years. All three are gone. What I wrote, what I made, what I was thinking at the time — none of it survived. I did not delete them; they died. A service shut down, a theme update broke things, a database did not migrate, and while setting up the new site I could not be bothered to import the old one.

Meanwhile info.cern.ch, built in 1991, still opens. My two-year-old site does not. The difference is not skill. It is the number of dependencies.

I looked at what actually died

In all three cases the content lived in a database. The database lived inside a service. The service belonged to a company, and the company to a pricing decision. Seeing a page required a running program, and the day the program stopped running, the page stopped existing.

The real problem: my content was never something I could hold. I could not open a folder and look at it. Even a backup was useless without a system that could read it back.

What is different this time

The page you are reading is a file on my disk, as it is. It was not compiled, not generated, not pulled from a database. Download the repository, double-click the file, and you get this exact page. You need no server, no internet, and no me.

Four rules:

  1. The browser opens a file; it does not execute a program.
  2. No published address ever dies.
  3. Tools are mortal, the site is not. The build script, the CI, the chat service — if all of them vanish tomorrow, the site still stands.
  4. Nothing gets deleted. If I change my mind, the history stays.

The fourth rule has a consequence: every page has a history page. If I edit this piece three years from now, the old version stays there. Git already keeps it, but the .git folder is not served to visitors and is not human-readable; if history is meant to be browsable, it has to be HTML.

The cost

There is no admin panel. Writing a new entry means creating a file. I do not count that as a loss — I am a developer, my editor is already open. Building a panel, then maintaining the panel, then losing access to my content when the panel breaks: that was the shared story of the three sites I lost.

There are no visual ambitions either. Times New Roman, blue links, eight lines of CSS. The fewer aesthetic decisions, the less there is to go stale.
